Cascade prediction for city lifelines

Catastrophes are too rare to learn from. Near-misses are the same process, stopped early.

A city cascade happens three times a decade, so there is nothing to train on. But every cascade that died after two hops used the same edges, the same delays, the same physics — and those sit unlabelled in every SCADA historian, precisely because nobody was hurt.

This is the gap the field leaves open. I³ (Tsinghua, 2025) and PI-GN-JODE (2026) both predict cascades well. Neither selects an intervention, and both train on synthetic scenario sweeps rather than mined history. The literature predicts the cascade; nobody picks the firebreak.

ROC-AUC on held-out scenarios, fresh seeds. The line marks chance (0.500). Anomaly score sits below it — ranking alerts by how loud they are is slightly worse than a coin.

Three integration bugs were found by measuring, not by reading. Flow-sibling relations have no edge object, so the estimator and the consumer derived different keys and 80% of the propagation mass silently vanished — H1 read exactly 0.500 with no error raised. spectral_radius was computing a max row sum and read 10.1 on a system already measured as subcritical. And delays are under-estimated by window truncation, which means intervention deadlines will read optimistic — safe direction, but stated now rather than discovered on stage.

Status at one day of three

What stands up, and what does not yet

Working

  • Five-layer city twin — power, water, transport, telecom, health, with buffers and restoration routed over the damaged road network
  • Cascade engine — load redistribution plus buffered dependency drawdown; delay emerges from physics, never from a config constant
  • Near-miss miner — chains recovered at 0.83 precision without ever seeing ground truth
  • Hawkes kernel + branching ratio — validated against a known synthetic kernel first

Not yet

  • Counterfactual intervention search — the part no paper does, and the one that makes the rewind demo work
  • The seven-arm ablation — including I³ run unmodified as a baseline rather than a strawman
  • Live console — this page renders exported data; the operator view is next
  • Real historian feed — everything above runs on a simulated city
What we need from you

Two things, and neither is encouragement

The method stands or falls on whether near-misses exist in real operational data the way they exist in ours, and on whether the intervention costs mean anything to someone who has actually had to make the call.

01 — Data

An introduction to one utility or municipal SCADA historian feed. We need event timestamps and asset IDs. Nothing that identifies a person, no appliance-level data, no continuous telemetry.

02 — Judgement

Half an hour with someone who has sat in a control room during a monsoon. Our intervention cost model is abstract units, and that is the part we cannot get right from outside.

Honest limitations, ours before yours: power and water asset topology is synthesised and geo-anchored because it is not public; layer physics is a capacity-flow resolver, with wntr and pandapower behind an interface for later; flood exposure uses latitude as a proxy for elevation; and the system is subcritical, so absolute cascade frequencies are not calibrated to a real city — every result here is a relative comparison on one simulator.
